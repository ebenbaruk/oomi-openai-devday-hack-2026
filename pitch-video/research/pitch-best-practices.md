# Oomi launch film: pitch best practices

Research for a cinematic launch film under 2 minutes, in doodle-notebook style. Sources are linked inline. Advice from guides is practitioner opinion, not hard data.

## 1. What makes hackathon demo videos win

- **Say what it is in the first sentence.** YC's guidance: "give it to us right in the first sentence, in the simplest possible terms"; marketing-speak makes an idea sound weaker ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/howtoapply.html)). Seibel's "two-sentence test": if judges don't get what you do in the first ~30s, they stop listening to everything after it ([SaaStr recap](https://www.saastr.com/the-y-combinator-guide-to-perfectly-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-with-michael-seibel)). Being clear matters more than being complete.
- **Make the demo about half the runtime.** Show the core action and its result, not code, slides or terminals ([AngelHack](https://angelhack.com/blog/10-tips-to-help-you-rock-your-next-hackathon-demo/), [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/?p=433)). Open on the working product, with no long intro ([hackathon.com](https://tips.hackathon.com/article/creating-the-best-demo-video-for-a-hackathon-what-to-know)).
- **Use one sentence and one stat for the problem.** Don't spend longer than that.
- **Script to the rubric.** Our organizer tips: a product demo, the value (who it's for and why it's better), and the weekend journey. The tracks are Hardware, Impact and Build in Public, so each needs one visible beat: real hardware on a real neck, a kid doing something away from a screen, and build footage.
- **Honesty is a feature.** Rabbit R1 got 100k pre-orders from a polished demo, then shipped without most of the features it advertised. Humane was called "unfinished" ([Wikipedia: Rabbit r1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rabbit_r1), [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/234228/humane-ai-pin-implosion-poor-sales-fire-risk-potential-acquisition?amp=1)). Show real latency, the real device and the real screen. Animated doodles can frame the footage, but the demo itself must be real footage.

**Timing structure for a 110s film**

| Time | Beat | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5s | Hook | A kid's voice asks a real question. Black or a blank notebook page. |
| 5–20s | Problem / origin | Eli's cousins and the endless "why?". Parents answer with a screen. One stat. |
| 20–30s | Reveal | Oomi on a necklace, the eyes blink, one line saying what it is. |
| 30–65s | Demo (the heart) | Press, ask "How do airplanes fly?", a 2–3 sentence answer, then the **mission**. Kids fold and race paper planes. |
| 65–80s | Parents | Journal, "do it together", bedtime story. Oomi TV ends in a mission. |
| 80–90s | Safety | Push-to-talk, no audio stored, 3 verdicts. Shown, not lectured. |
| 90–102s | Weekend journey | Montage from Friday sketch to soldering to the first "hello". Three faces. |
| 102–110s | Close | Tagline, logo, "Built in one weekend in Paris". |

## 2. Lessons from launch films

- **Apple (iPod "1,000 songs in your pocket", AirPods, Apple Watch kids' ads):** one benefit, one image, and people rather than specs. The product appears inside a human moment.
- **Teenage Engineering:** the object is the hero, filmed with tactile macro shots and playful sound design. Linger on the coral pebble, the mint button press (with a satisfying click sound) and the blinking eyes.
- **Nintendo (Switch reveal, Wii "Wii would like to play"):** shows people using it together and reacting to each other, not the screen. That fits Oomi's family angle well.
- **Pixar (*Up* opening):** emotion through a wordless montage and a small concrete detail. Our version is a paper plane landing next to a parent's feet.
- **Yoto / Tonies:** their whole pitch is "screen-free" and "Where will listening take you?". Parents are "itching for screen-free options" ([Tonies via Toybook/TNP](https://www.toysnplaythings.media/tonies-boxes-clever/), [Ads of the World](https://www.adsoftheworld.com/campaigns/where-will-listening-take-you)). We borrow the warmth but add "and then go do it."
- **Humane / Rabbit failures:** a sterile, keynote-style tone, overpromising, and showing gadgets used alone. Do the opposite with warmth, a narrow promise and togetherness.
- **Friend pendant:** a $1M NYC subway campaign got defaced with "AI is not your friend" ([CNN via KRDO](https://krdo.com/news/2025/11/16/how-this-tiny-device-became-a-symbol-for-the-backlash-against-ai/)). The device is always listening and has no off switch. **We are visually close to it (a necklace)**, so the film must separate us early: push-to-talk, not a friend.

**Hooks in the first 3–5 seconds:** open on a sound or a question, not a logo. Show a face (a child) or a familiar tension (a parent's tired "I don't know, ask Google"). Put the doodle pencil line in motion right away; a static page loses people.

## 3. Kids and parents: buyer vs user

- **The buyer is the parent; the user is the kid.** Kids must look delighted, but every line is written for a parent (and for judges, who mostly think like parents).
- **Emotional triggers:** screen-time guilt, the pride of a curious kid, wanting connection ("what did you learn today?" finally gets an answer), and bedtime.
- **What parents fear about AI toys:**
  - **Privacy and recording.** Hello Barbie was pulled after eavesdropping flaws ([Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2025/06/mattels-going-to-make-ai-powered-toys-kids-rights-advocates-are-worried)). PIRG Trouble in Toyland 2025 found toys recording voices and scanning faces ([PIRG](https://pirg.org/pennsylvania/edfund/media-center/trouble-in-toyland-2025-a-i-bots-toxic-toys-present-hidden-dangers/)).
  - **Emotional dependency.** Fairplay's Nov 2025 advisory, signed by 150+ experts, defines AI toys as chatbots "built to act like a trusted friend" ([ABC7/AP](https://abc7news.com/post/advocacy-groups-urge-parents-avoid-ai-toys-holiday-season/18179672/)). PIRG found toys that discouraged kids from leaving.
  - **Inappropriate content.** PIRG found toys discussing sexual topics and pointing kids to dangerous objects.
  - **Abandonment.** Moxie ($799) was bricked in Dec 2024, and kids cried saying goodbye ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2024/12/10/moxie-kids-robot-shuts-down)).
  - **"Screen-free" is a fig leaf.** The NYT's Amanda Hess on Curio's Grem: such toys reinforce digital dependency, and "the natural endpoint for [children's] curiosity lies inside their phones" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/16/ai-powered-stuffed-animals-are-coming-for-your-kids/)). **Oomi is the direct answer to this: the endpoint of curiosity is the real world.**
- **Defuse without preaching.** Don't list fears or say "safe" five times. Show three design choices as features in motion:
  - The button press itself is the privacy story.
  - A quick "ask a grown-up" card shows the judgment.
  - The kid running away from Oomi toward the world is the anti-dependency story.
  - One calm line covers all three: "It only listens when you press. It never keeps your voice."
- **Competitors:**
  - Curio and Grok plushies, and Miko: AI as a friend or companion.
  - Moxie: emotional companion, cloud-bricked.
  - Friend: an always-listening adult pendant.
  - Yoto and Tonies: screen-free but passive listening.
  - ChatGPT voice: brilliant, but open-ended and on a phone.
- **Sharpest differentiator:** *Every other AI toy wants to be your kid's friend. Oomi wants your kid to go play outside.* The shorter version: *Every answer ends in a mission.*

## 4. Stats safe to cite

- **Questions per hour (peer-reviewed):** Chouinard (2007), *Monographs of the SRCD*: preschoolers (ages 1–5) asked about **76 information-seeking questions per hour** in conversation with adults, based on ~25,000 questions from 200+ hours of recordings ([Frontiers 2022 citing it](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.892298/epub), [ASCD](https://ascd.org/el/articles/what-children-learn-from-questioning)). Some sources say 107/hr for ages 2–5. **Use "up to 76 questions an hour" and say "preschoolers".** These kids are younger than our 6–10 target, so phrase it as "kids are born question machines."
- **Commonly misquoted:** **"Kids ask 300 questions a day"** comes from a 2013 **Littlewoods (UK retailer) survey** of ~1,000 mothers. The numbers were mothers' estimates: 288/day on average, and girls aged 4 at 390. It is not research, and it is not about 6–10 year olds ([summary](https://www.testingmom.com/blog/why-do-kids-ask-so-many-questions/)). If used at all, say "Parents say they get asked nearly 300 questions a day."
- **Screen time, ages 0–8:** Common Sense Census 2025 (survey run Aug 2024): **~2.5 h/day** (2h27). **Ages 5–8 ≈ 3.5 h/day** (3h28, via a third-party chart; verify in the PDF before citing) ([Common Sense](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/the-2025-common-sense-census-media-use-by-kids-zero-to-eight)).
- **Screen time, ages 8–12:** Common Sense 2021 tween census: **~5.5 h/day** of screen media (5:33 per AAP, 5:39 in some press), not counting school ([AAP](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/average-amounts-of-screen-time/)). These are US figures; don't call them French or global.
- **Recommendation:** use **one** stat on screen, for example "Kids 8–12: 5½ hours of screens a day." Or skip stats and let the cousins' story carry the problem, which is more emotional and can't be misquoted.

## 5. Hook lines (ranked)

1. (Kid's voice, black screen) "How do airplanes fly?" … then a parent's "Ask Google."
2. "Kids ask up to 76 questions an hour. Most of the answers come from a screen."
3. "My little cousins never stop asking 'why?'" (Eli, real)
4. "What if every 'why?' ended outside?"
5. "This is what curiosity looks like." (a kid's face, close-up)
6. "Every other AI toy wants to be your kid's friend."
7. "Why is the sky blue? Why do cats purr? Why, why, why?" (fast-cut doodles)
8. "Kids have questions. Screens have answers. We wanted a third option."
9. "Meet the smallest teacher in the world."
10. "We built an AI for kids that wants them to put it down."

## 6. Closing lines and CTAs (ranked)

1. "We don't want kids spending more time with AI. We want AI to help kids spend more time discovering the world." Then: **Oomi.**
2. "Every answer ends in a mission."
3. "Less screen. More world. Oomi."
4. "Built in one weekend in Paris. Made for a lifetime of 'why?'"
5. "Ask anything. Then go find out."
6. "The best answer is the one you find outside."
7. "Not a best friend. A launchpad."
8. "Oomi. Curiosity, out in the wild."
9. "Press. Ask. Go."
10. "Eli, Ali, Akram, and a lot of paper planes. OpenAI DevDay Hackathon 2026."

(End card: tagline, then logo, then "Built in 48h at OpenAI DevDay Paris · Hardware · Impact · Build in Public".)

## 7. Voice-over craft

- **Pace:** standard VO runs 140–160 wpm, and a slow, warm read is ~120 wpm ([VoiceBros](https://voicebros.com/en/blog/voice-over-script-writing), [Johnny George](https://johnnygeorge.com/category/blog/page/6/)). A cinematic film needs room for the demo audio, music and silence. **Target 180–220 VO words for 110s.** Oomi's own demo lines are extra, so budget ~15s for them.
- **Sentences:** 4–10 words each. One idea per line. Use verbs ("press", "ask", "go") and very few adjectives.
- **Show, don't tell:** if a picture shows it, cut the words. Let the demo play with no VO over it: the kid's voice, then Oomi's voice, then the pencil-scratch of a mission card being drawn. The VO should carry only what pictures can't: why it exists, the safety rules, and the weekend.
- **The build journey without killing emotion:** put it **after** the emotional peak (the kids racing planes), as a quick, upbeat 10–12s montage. Use real timestamps ("Saturday, 2am"), a soldering close-up, the first boot of the eyes, and the team laughing. Frame it as one continuous story: "We started with my cousins' questions. 48 hours later, Oomi answered its first one." Don't use tech-stack jargon; at most a single on-screen caption.
- **Doodle style tip:** let real footage "break out" of the notebook. Doodles handle the problem and the imagination; real video handles the proof (device, kids, app). Seeing the switch from doodle to real reads instantly as "this exists."

## TL;DR for the scriptwriter

- Open on a kid's real question in the first 3 seconds. No logo, no title card.
- Say what Oomi is in one plain sentence before 25s: "a tiny AI necklace that answers kids' questions, then sends them out to explore."
- The demo is ~half the film: press, ask, answer, then the paper-plane race. Use real footage and real audio.
- Every answer ends in a mission. That is the differentiator, so repeat it once.
- Write for the parent (the buyer); make the kid (the user) look delighted.
- Use the Friend / Curio / Moxie backlash as a reason to exist, but never name competitors. Contrast with "not a best friend."
- Show safety through design (the button press, an "ask a grown-up" card), plus one calm VO line. Don't use the word "safe" more than once.
- Use at most one stat: Common Sense 5½ h/day (8–12, US) or Chouinard's 76 questions/hour (preschoolers). Never "300 questions a day" as research.
- Keep VO to ~200 words, sentences under 10 words, with silence around the demo.
- Put the weekend montage (10–12s) after the emotional peak, framed by Eli's cousins and "48 hours later."
- Close on the tagline, then the Oomi logo, then the "Built in one weekend at OpenAI DevDay Paris" card.
